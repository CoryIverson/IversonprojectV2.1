---
title: Alfred Wallace 
---
Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913) was a British naturalist who spent years collecting and studying species across the world. Unlike Darwin, who came from a afluent family and upbringing, Wallace grew up with fewer resources and built his scientific career through fieldwork and specimen collecting. His observations of geographic distribution and variation led him to ideas strikingly similar to Darwin’s.

When Wallace sent his 1858 manuscript *On the Tendency of Varieties to Depart Indefinitely From the Original Type* to Charles Darwin, Darwin was stunned by how closely it mirrored his own unpublished theory, which caused Darwin to frantically scramble to work on his own theory and reach out to others. Unsure how to proceed without wronging either man, Darwin's friends, colleagues, and influential leaders of their respective societies and communities Charles Lyell and Joseph Hooker organized a joint presentation at the Linnean Society. Wallace’s paper and excerpts from Darwin’s earlier writings were read together for a joint authorship of the theory. With the support from Thomas Henry Huxley and others, Darwin and Wallace were recognized simultaneously without direct rivalry to avoid disputes. Although the Linnean Society presentation effectively made them co-authors of natural selection, Wallace never contested Darwin’s priority over the decades but remained critical in the development of the evolutionary discourse. This is one small example of how this discourse could have changed drastically if Wallace had never sent his manuscript prior to Darwin, or if Darwin had not made such powerful social connections.
